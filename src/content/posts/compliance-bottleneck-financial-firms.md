Walk into almost any financial firm and you'll see the same pattern: compliance lives with a small group, and everyone else works around it. Advisors pause before sending client communications. Marketing teams wait days for approvals. Employees guess when they're unsure what the policy allows, not because they don't care about compliance, but because they don't have real access to it.

### The Friction Is Hiding in Plain Sight

You don't need to look hard to find the bottlenecks. A marketing manager drafts a campaign, sends it for review, and watches it sit in a queue alongside dozens of other requests. By the time feedback comes back, it's late and requires multiple rounds of revision. Meanwhile, an advisor who wants to share a timely market update hesitates, unsure if the language crosses a line, and either waits for guidance or skips the communication altogether. These aren't edge cases. They're daily occurrences across broker-dealers and RIAs, and they add up fast. Firms typically respond the way you'd expect: more reviewers, more policies, more systems. But the outcome rarely improves. Decisions slow down, compliance teams get buried, and employees grow frustrated. Industry data reflects this — around [85 percent of executives](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/issues/risk-regulation/global-compliance-survey.html) report that compliance requirements have become more complex in recent years, and 77 percent say that complexity has negatively impacted their ability to grow. Separately, [PwC's 27th Global CEO Survey](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/issues/risk-regulation/global-compliance-survey.html) found that 64 percent of CEOs consider the regulatory environment the number one barrier to reinvention and value creation. The complexity is real. But it's not actually the root problem.

### The Real Issue Is Structural

What’s missing isn’t more oversight—it’s access. And without access, compliance is hard to democratize. Most compliance programs follow the same basic structure: a small group of experts owns the knowledge, makes decisions, and approves actions. Every question, review, and escalation flows through them. New technology rarely changes this. Most systems are designed to record activity, not distribute knowledge or enable people to act. The burden stays where it has always been, and everyone else waits. The predictable side effect is disengagement. People stop interacting with compliance directly. They defer decisions instead of making them. Over time, they begin working *around* compliance rather than *with* it. [Research from Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2018/03/why-compliance-programs-fail) highlights this pattern. Programs built around control and enforcement fail to influence behavior across the organization. Employees follow rules when required, but they don't internalize them. Data from NAVEX Global's [2025 State of Risk & Compliance Report](https://www.navex.com/en-us/northstar/global-risk-compliance-statistics/), a survey of nearly 1,000 professionals conducted with The Harris Poll, reinforces this trend. Only 47 percent of respondents said building an ethical compliance culture was a top decision-making consideration, down from 76 percent just two years earlier. In a financial firm, that gap matters. Compliance depends on consistent, everyday decisions made by people outside the compliance team. If those people are disengaged, the system weakens.

### The Cost Is Cultural, Not Just Operational

Most firms measure compliance through policies, audits, and controls. But compliance shows up in everyday decisions. Those decisions don't improve through static documents or after-the-fact reviews. They improve through access. [Research from PwC](https://www.pwc.de/de/risk-regulatory/global-compliance-survey/pwc-global-compliance-study-2025-fs-industry-lens.pdf) reinforces this. In financial services, the factors considered most important in building a strong compliance culture are senior management sponsorship and employee training and communication, with a clear emphasis on embedding compliance into daily decision-making.⁵ When employees can't easily find answers and lack confidence in their decisions, compliance stays reactive. Culture doesn't evolve.

### The Complexity Trap

As regulation expands, firms respond in predictable ways: more rules, more layers of control, more systems. [Nearly 90 percent of organizations](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/issues/risk-regulation/global-compliance-survey.html) report that the breadth of compliance responsibilities has increased in the last three years, a figure that rises to [90 percent in financial services](https://www.pwc.de/de/risk-regulatory/global-compliance-survey/pwc-global-compliance-study-2025-fs-industry-lens.pdf) specifically. The [Thomson Reuters Institute's 2025 C-Suite Survey](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/institute.html) confirms the downstream effect. C-suite executives identified time-consuming compliance and reporting tasks as a significant constraint on meeting broader business objectives. But as complexity grows, participation declines. The harder compliance is to understand, the more organizations centralize it. The more they centralize it, the fewer people can engage with it meaningfully. As participation drops, breakdowns increase. The result is a self-reinforcing cycle: complexity drives centralization, centralization drives disengagement, and disengagement drives risk.

**The issue isn't complexity alone. It's the lack of access needed to navigate it.**

### A More Accessible, Democratized Model Is Emerging

Firms that move faster and operate more effectively are shifting their approach. They are not focused only on tracking activity or adding oversight. They are expanding participation across the organization. In practice, the difference is clear. Instead of submitting a question and waiting hours or days for a compliance officer to respond, an advisor checks guidance in the moment, before an email is sent or a meeting takes place. Instead of sending drafts into a review queue, marketing teams understand what is compliant before they submit. Compliance becomes something people use, not something they wait on. This shift changes compliance from a gate into a guide. It supports better decisions in real time instead of slowing work after the fact. This is what it means to democratize compliance: making it accessible across the organization, not just managed from the center.

### What Changes When Compliance Is Actually Accessible

When compliance is no longer confined to a single department, the compliance team's role evolves. They still define policies, handle complex cases, and provide oversight, but they're no longer the chokepoint for every routine decision. The result is fewer delays, more consistency, and stronger accountability. More importantly, it changes how people across the organization actually behave. Employees stop guessing and start acting with confidence, because they have the access to do so.

### The Bottom Line

**Compliance breaks down when knowledge and decisions are centralized in a small team instead of accessible across the organization.**

It doesn't have to work this way. Compliance can exist across the firm, embedded in daily work and supported by systems that provide clarity when it's needed. This isn't a theoretical shift. It's already happening at firms that recognize a simple truth: access, not just oversight, determines how effective a compliance program can be. Systems that only record activity don't improve decisions. They document problems after they happen.

So the question is simple: if your compliance infrastructure isn't helping people act in the moment, what role is it really playing?
